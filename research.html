Beyond the headline figures

What needs a fuller assessment?

These are questions for investigation. They are not a finding that every program creates each harm, or that burning has a negative net outcome nationally.

  1. Who benefits, who is exposed, and who can afford protection?

    Map fire protection and smoke exposure across the whole affected area. Ask whether households, outdoor workers, schools and aged-care facilities can act on warnings, and who pays for filtration, safe indoor space or property upgrades. RFS guidance on smoke and susceptible groups ↗

  2. Water, soils and catchments

    Assess sediment, nutrients and recovery after planned fire alongside avoided wildfire effects. A direct Victorian study found only minor effects from its burns; severity, burn pattern and rainfall still matter. Read the catchment study ↓

  3. The full carbon balance

    Compare treatment emissions, avoided wildfire emissions and changes in living vegetation, dead wood and soil over time. Emission-only and full carbon-pool models can give different results. Read both modelling approaches ↓

  4. Operational feasibility and crew exposure

    Include suitable weather windows, escapes, coordination, staffing and maintenance. Assess smoke exposure during planned work alongside safer conditions for later suppression. This page has not estimated occupational illness or escape rates. CSIRO on practical benefits and limits ↗

  5. What happens between a burn and the next wildfire?

    Look beyond the immediate fuel reduction to regrowth, habitat recovery, drought and interactions with the next fire. Effects on animals can also follow changes in shelter and predator pressure. Local findings need local application. Read Price’s research synthesis ↓ · Read the forest-age study, comment and reply ↓

  6. What counts as success, and who checks it?

    Ask for observed protection and exposure, prediction accuracy, uncertainty and alternatives alongside hectares treated. Trace commissioners and reused assumptions through to policy. An independent assessment should bring health, ecology, economics and operations together. Read the analysis of research gaps ↓ · Read the commissioned reports ↓

Every source from the briefing

The report’s 27 references.

Grouped by question for easier reading. The reference numbers match the PDF. Agency pages and commissioned reports are labelled separately from academic papers.

Protection, targeting & alternatives

Report reference 3 · Peer-reviewed research

Gibbons P, Gale MG, Moritz MA, Cary GJ (2025). The effectiveness of prescribed burning for protecting houses during wildfires in Australia. Fire Ecology 21:59. ↗

House-loss associations, placement and defensible space.

Read with this limit: Observational house-loss study. The recent-fire measure includes prescribed fire and wildfire; it does not calculate national net benefit.

Report reference 12 · Peer-reviewed research

Florec V, Burton M, Pannell D, Kelso J, Milne G (2020; published online 2019). Where to prescribe burn: the costs and benefits of prescribed burning close to houses. International Journal of Wildland Fire 29(5):440-458. ↗

Separate economic study of burn placement in south-west WA.

Read with this limit: A separate economic model of burn placement in south-west WA; do not attribute Penman’s 11-region result to this paper.

Report reference 25 · Research database

CSIRO. Understanding loss of life in bushfires. ↗

1901-2011 fatality database and its scope.

Read with this limit: Historical fatality database, 1901–2011. It is not a controlled test of burning effectiveness.

Report reference 26 · Government trial report

NSW Department of Primary Industries (2024). Final Report on the Mechanical Fuel Load Reduction Trials. PUB24/548. Published on the Australian Government agriculture department website. ↗

Three forest trial sites; treatment impacts and simulation-based risk assessment.

Read with this limit: Three forest trial sites with varied effects. Risk estimates use simulations; retained treatment debris can increase surface fuel.

Report reference 13 · Agency guidance

NSW Rural Fire Service. Prepare your home. ↗

Home preparation and links to bushfire survival planning.

Read with this limit: Practical official preparation advice, rather than a treatment-effect study.

Smoke, health & exposure

Report reference 1 · Peer-reviewed research

Broome RA, Johnston FH, Horsley J, Morgan GG (2016). A rapid assessment of the impact of hazard reduction burning around Sydney, May 2016. Medical Journal of Australia 205(9):407-408. ↗

Planned-burn smoke episode and uncertainty.

Read with this limit: A short Sydney episode; deaths are population estimates with uncertainty, not identified individual deaths or a toll per burn.

Report reference 2 · Peer-reviewed research

Horsley JA et al. (2018). Health burden associated with fire smoke in Sydney, 2001-2013. Medical Journal of Australia 208(7):309-310. ↗

Combined landscape-fire smoke health estimates.

Read with this limit: Combines landscape-fire smoke sources; cannot be cited as an HRB-only burden.

Report reference 16 · Peer-reviewed research

Borchers-Arriagada N et al. (2021). Smoke health costs and the calculus for wildfires fuel management: a modelling study. The Lancet Planetary Health 5:e608-e619. ↗

NSW 2000-2020; prescribed-fire and wildfire smoke health costs separately attributed.

Read with this limit: Historical NSW source-attributed health costs. Does not compare the future net smoke burden of alternative management strategies.

Report reference 17 · Peer-reviewed research

Yoon HJ, Boyle J, Diouf I, Jayasena R (2026). The Impact of Heat and Bushfire Smoke on Health System Utilisation in Australia. Medical Journal of Australia 224(8), e70261. ↗

Published 6 August 2026; public abstract checked; short-term associations, 2014-2020.

Read with this limit: Heat and bushfire-smoke associations with health-system use; not an HRB-only study. The review used the public abstract.

Report reference 24 · Peer-reviewed research

Hertzog L et al. (2024). Mortality burden attributable to exceptional PM2.5 air pollution events in Australian cities: A health impact assessment. Heliyon 10:e24532. ↗

Exceptional-pollution mortality estimate across cities; not a hazard-reduction-only estimate.

Read with this limit: Exceptional pollution events in Australian cities; the estimated burden is not confined to HRB smoke.

Report reference 11 · Agency guidance

NSW Rural Fire Service. Smoke from hazard reduction. ↗

Agency account of smoke risks, susceptible groups and public resources.

Read with this limit: Official advice and agency description; not an independent evaluation of program effectiveness.

Report reference 14 · Agency guidance

NSW Rural Fire Service. Managing smoke and its impact on the community. ↗

Existing smoke planning, notification and assessment processes.

Read with this limit: Describes safeguards already in use. Their effectiveness needs measured outcomes as well as documented procedures.

Ecology, fire history & scientific disagreement

Report reference 5 · Peer-reviewed research

Driscoll DA et al. (2024). Biodiversity impacts of the 2019-2020 Australian megafires. Nature 635:898-905. ↗

Biodiversity responses and preceding fire history.

Read with this limit: Biodiversity responses to megafires and earlier fire history. Earlier fires were not all identified as prescribed burns.

Report reference 6 · Peer-reviewed research

Zylstra PJ, Bradshaw SD, Lindenmayer DB (2022). Self-thinning forest understoreys reduce wildfire risk, even in a warming climate. Environmental Research Letters 17:044022. ↗

WA fire history and time since fire; regional observational evidence.

Read with this limit: Regional observational analysis in south-west WA. Read the published methodological comment and reply below before generalising.

Report reference 7 · Peer-reviewed comment

Miller BP et al. (2024). Comment on "Self-thinning forest understoreys reduce wildfire risk, even in a warming climate". Environmental Research Letters 19:068001. ↗

Published critique of the statistical analysis.

Read with this limit: A peer-reviewed methodological challenge to reference 6; a comment is not an independent field experiment.

Report reference 8 · Peer-reviewed reply

Zylstra PJ, Lindenmayer DB, Bradshaw SD (2024). Reply to Comment on "Self-thinning forest understoreys reduce wildfire risk, even in a warming climate". Environmental Research Letters 19:058001. ↗

Authors' response to the critique; author-uploaded full text consulted.

Read with this limit: The authors contest the criticism in reference 7. A reply does not, by itself, settle the disagreement.

Report reference 9 · Peer-reviewed research

Canadell JG et al. (2021). Multi-decadal increase of forest burned area in Australia is linked to climate change. Nature Communications 12:6921. ↗

Historical prescribed-burning trends in studied forest ecosystems.

Read with this limit: Climate and historical forest-fire trends; not a controlled comparison of contemporary HRB strategies.

Report reference 20 · Government experimental research report

Department of Sustainability and Environment, Victoria (2003). Ecological effects of repeated low-intensity fire in mixed eucalypt foothill forest in south-eastern Australia: Summary report (1984-1999). Research Report 57. ↗

Wombat experiment; vegetation pp.16-23; Hopmans soil chapter pp.58-64 (PDF pp.72-78).

Read with this limit: Direct prescribed-fire experiment with mixed responses by treatment. Neither universal ecological collapse nor universal ecological safety follows.

Report reference 21 · Peer-reviewed research

Bradshaw SD, Bradshaw FJ (2017). Long-term recovery from fire by a population of honey possums (Tarsipes rostratus) in the extreme south-west of Western Australia. Australian Journal of Zoology 65:1-11. ↗

Publisher abstract checked; 25.6 years is a regression-based recovery projection.

Read with this limit: Species- and region-specific. The reported 25.6-year recovery is a regression projection, not an exact universal recovery time.

Report reference 22 · Peer-reviewed research

Valentine LE et al. (2014). Time since fire influences food resources for an endangered species, Carnaby's cockatoo, in a fire-prone landscape. Biological Conservation 175:1-9. ↗

Primary author-uploaded paper consulted: seed measurements and energetic carrying-capacity model.

Read with this limit: Seed-resource measurements and an energetic carrying-capacity model; not a measured national population effect.

Report reference 23 · Peer-reviewed perspective

Bradshaw SD et al. (2026). Likely substantial impacts of Western Australian government prescribed fire policies on biodiversity in a global hotspot. Australian Journal of Zoology 74:ZO26011. ↗

Perspective, published 10 July 2026; author-uploaded full paper checked; some supporting research is in press.

Read with this limit: A perspective on WA policy, not one new controlled experiment. Some supporting research was in press when published.

Report reference 10 · Secondary fact check

RMIT ABC Fact Check (22 January 2020; updated 5 February 2020). Has NSW seen more than twice the amount of prescribed burning in national parks this decade compared with the last? ↗

Secondary verification of mapped NPWS data; not a current exposure series.

Read with this limit: A historical fact check of mapped NPWS data, not a current national burn or smoke-exposure dataset.

Economic models, commissioning & policy

Report reference 4 · Peer-reviewed research

Penman TD, Clarke H, Cirulis B, Boer MM, Price OF, Bradstock RA (2020). Cost-Effective Prescribed Burning Solutions Vary Between Landscapes in Eastern Australia. Frontiers in Forests and Global Change 3:79. ↗

Regional model results, valued assets and limitations.

Read with this limit: Regional simulation and economic modelling. Population smoke impacts were explicitly outside scope; results vary between landscapes.

Report reference 18 · Commissioned economic scoping report

Deloitte Access Economics (2014). Scoping study on a cost benefit analysis of bushfire mitigation. Prepared for the Australian Forest Products Association. ↗

Commissioning: introduction; illustrative Blue Mountains assumptions: printed pp.43-45 (PDF pp.47-49).

Read with this limit: Industry-commissioned scoping study combining burning and biomass removal. Illustrative assumptions and returns are not observed HRB-only results.

Report reference 19 · Commissioned economic report

Insurance Council of Australia / Finity Consulting (2022). Reaping the rewards of resilience. ↗

Fuel management: technical appendix pp.3-6; health/social assumptions: pp.10-11; headline investment: PDF p.8.

Read with this limit: Insurer-commissioned assessment of mixed fuel-management investment. Reuse of earlier Deloitte inputs is not independent validation.

Report reference 15 · Government partnership information

National Emergency Management Agency. Hazards Insurance Partnership. ↗

Official purpose of government-insurer cooperation.

Read with this limit: Documents government–insurer cooperation and its stated purpose; not a study of HRB effectiveness.

Report reference 27 · Organisation website

Australian Business Roundtable for Disaster Resilience and Safer Communities. Official website. ↗

Founding organisations and stated role of reports and advocacy in policy.

Read with this limit: Documents openly stated organisational advocacy and membership, rather than demonstrating hidden sponsorship.

Beyond the report bibliography

Additional papers & synthesis.

These extend the questions covered on the website. They are additional reading, not newly inserted references in the report PDF.

Additional reading · Research synthesis / free chapter PDF

Owen Price (2023). Prescribed burning in Australian forests: characteristics, impacts and effects. In Australia’s Megafires, chapter 21, pp.273–284. ↗

A useful overview of protection, biodiversity and practical limits. Describes benefits from targeted burning and concerns about more frequent treatment and climate-driven expansion.

Read with this limit: A synthesis spanning different evidence types; its summaries and ratios are not a universal estimate for every forest or burn.

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed literature analysis / open access

Gordon L, Evans MJ, Zylstra P, Lindenmayer DB (2025). Trends and Gaps in Prescribed Burning Research. Environmental Management 75:746–760. ↗

Analyses 7,878 articles and identifies geographic concentration and gaps between disciplines. Useful for asking whether health, ecology and forest management are considered together.

Read with this limit: Maps research coverage, not the net effectiveness of HRB. It does not establish who funded the entire literature or prove manipulation.

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed catchment study

Smith HG, Sheridan GJ, Lane PNJ, Sherwin CB (2010). Paired Eucalyptus forest catchment study of prescribed fire effects on suspended sediment and nutrient exports in south-eastern Australia. International Journal of Wildland Fire 19:624–636. ↗

Two Victorian catchments provide direct prescribed-fire evidence. The burns examined had only a very minor effect on sediment and nutrient exports.

Read with this limit: Patchy, mostly low-severity burns and local rainfall conditions matter; this does not establish negligible effects in every catchment.

Author-uploaded full paper ↗

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed emissions model

Bradstock RA et al. (2012). Modelling the potential for prescribed burning to mitigate carbon emissions from wildfires in fire-prone forests of Australia. International Journal of Wildland Fire 21:629–639. ↗

The simulations suggest limited potential for net emissions reductions in the temperate eucalypt forests studied; burning more can add emissions even when average fire intensity falls.

Read with this limit: Modelled emissions, not a complete measured ecosystem carbon balance. Read the later carbon-pool model below as well.

University record and abstract ↗

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed ecosystem carbon model

Volkova L, Roxburgh SH, Weston CJ (2021). Effects of prescribed fire frequency on wildfire emissions and carbon sequestration in a fire adapted ecosystem using a comprehensive carbon model. Journal of Environmental Management 290:112673. ↗

Includes carbon storage as well as emissions. Under its assumptions, increased prescribed-fire emissions were approximately offset by reduced wildfire losses, with limited abatement and no significant emissions penalty.

Read with this limit: A model for the studied ecosystem, not a national accounting result. Different carbon pools and assumptions help explain why emission-only comparisons can differ.

University record and manuscript link ↗Public abstract ↗

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed monitoring study / open access

Price OF, Forehead H (2021). Smoke Patterns around Prescribed Fires in Australian Eucalypt Forests, as Measured by Low-Cost Particulate Monitors. Atmosphere 12:1389. ↗

Measurements around 18 Sydney-region burns show how exposure changes with weather, distance and time of day. Evening pollution spread farther and could include areas upwind.

Read with this limit: A regional sample including three cultural burns. Its study-specific hourly threshold is not a current national health standard; observed distances are not a safe-distance rule.

University repository record ↗Publisher full text via ResearchGate ↗

Additional reading · Peer-reviewed governance study

Clement S, Garmestani A, Beckwith JA, Cannon PJ (2024). To burn or not to burn: governance of wildfires in Australia. Ecology and Society 29(1):8. ↗

A route into research on how fire-management decisions are made, using policy material, visits and stakeholder interviews.

Read with this limit: Governance research is a different kind of evidence from fire-behaviour experiments or a calculation of net health benefit. Listed for further reading, not as proof of a quantified HRB effect.

Explanations, positions & public documents

Other resources worth reading.

These links have different purposes. A university news article explains a study; an organisation’s response records its position; an official strategy sets out a policy framework. None substitutes for the underlying methods.

CSIRO · Agency explanation · 2021

Why hazard reduction burning is no smokescreen ↗

Explains how fuel reduction can slow fire and assist suppression, why weather matters, and why burning is one tool among several. A useful starting point for understanding the practical case for HRB.

ANU · Research institution explanation · 2022

Coming of age: research on older forests and flammability ↗

A public explanation of the regional forest-age analysis. Read it with the paper, published criticism and authors’ reply; the headline is not a rule for all Australian forests.

Forestry Australia · Professional association response · 2025

Logging and prescribed burning do not make Australian forests more flammable ↗

The association’s response to claims about forest flammability, with links and references to supporting research. This is a professional position statement, not itself a new field experiment. The cited studies should be assessed on their own methods and landscapes.

NSW Government · Policy strategy · 2025

NSW Cultural Fire Strategy (PDF) ↗

Sets out a framework for Aboriginal-led cultural fire. Cultural authority and purposes deserve consideration in their own terms; cultural burning and agency hazard reduction should not be treated as interchangeable.

Indigenous-led organisation

Firesticks Alliance ↗

The organisation’s own account of cultural fire, community practice and learning. Included so readers can hear directly from an Indigenous-led organisation, rather than only through agency or academic descriptions.

Australian Government · Public inquiry · 2020

Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements ↗

Report gateway for the existing national inquiry. A new review should identify unanswered questions and build on public work already completed.
